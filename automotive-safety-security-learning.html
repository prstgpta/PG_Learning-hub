🚗 Safe & Secure Vehicles

Functional Safety (FuSa), Cybersecurity, HARA and TARA, explained simply

The big picture in one sentence

A modern car can hurt people in two ways: something inside it breaks, or someone breaks into it. Functional Safety handles the first. Cybersecurity handles the second.

🛡️ Functional Safety (FuSa)
"What if the electronics or software fail?"
Accidental. No bad intent. Example: a sensor wire breaks.
🔐 Cybersecurity
"What if someone attacks the car?"
Deliberate. A clever attacker. Example: a hacker sends fake brake commands.

What: The safety "risk check". List what could go wrong if a function fails, and how bad it would be.

Output: A safety level (ASIL A to D) and safety goals.

What: The security "risk check". List what attackers could target, how much damage it causes, and how easy the attack is.

Output: A risk value (1 to 5) and cybersecurity goals.

💼 Why leaders should care

July 2024
From this date, every new car registered in the EU must meet UN R155 (cybersecurity) and R156 (software updates). No certificate means no sales.
1.4 million
Vehicles recalled in 2015 after researchers remotely hacked a Jeep and controlled its steering and brakes over the mobile network.
ASIL D
The highest safety level (e.g., steering, braking). It drives major cost, time and supplier choices, so getting HARA right early saves money.

📅 Key standards & regulations (click to expand)

2011 / 2018: ISO 26262: Functional Safety for road vehicles
The main safety standard. 12 parts covering the full life of a product, from idea to scrapping. 2nd edition in 2018 added trucks, buses, motorcycles and semiconductors.
2021: ISO/SAE 21434: Cybersecurity engineering
Defines how to build security into vehicles across their whole life. Clause 15 describes the TARA method.
2021 to 2024: UNECE R155 & R156: Legal requirements
R155 requires a certified Cyber Security Management System (CSMS). R156 requires a Software Update Management System (SUMS). Mandatory in the EU for new vehicle types from July 2022 and all new vehicles from July 2024. Also adopted by Japan, Korea and others.
2022: ISO 21448 (SOTIF): Safety without failure
Covers hazards where nothing is broken but the system still behaves unsafely. Example: a camera blinded by the sun. Very important for driver-assist and self-driving features.
2024: ISO/PAS 8800: Safety of AI in vehicles
Guidance on making AI/machine-learning components safe enough for road vehicles.

✅ Key takeaways

1️⃣ Safety and security are partners. A hack on the brakes is a safety problem too.
2️⃣ Start early. HARA and TARA belong in the concept phase. Fixing late is expensive.
3️⃣ Security never ends. Cars need monitoring and updates for 15+ years.
4️⃣ It's a market-access issue. Without R155/R156 compliance, vehicles cannot be sold in many markets.

Module 1

Functional Safety (FuSa): ISO 26262

Official meaning: "Absence of unreasonable risk due to hazards caused by malfunctioning behaviour of E/E systems."
In plain words: If a car's electronics or software go wrong, nobody should get hurt.

Two kinds of failure

🐞 Systematic faults (design mistakes)
A bug or wrong requirement. It happens every time the same conditions occur.
Fixed by good process: clear requirements, reviews, coding rules (e.g., MISRA), testing.
🎲 Random hardware faults (parts wear out)
A chip, sensor or wire fails at a random time.
Handled by safety mechanisms: watchdogs, error-correcting memory (ECC), duplicate "lockstep" processors, plausibility checks.

🔗 Related standards: SOTIF and AI

ISO 21448 (SOTIF): "Safety Of The Intended Functionality". Covers danger when nothing is broken but the system's design isn't good enough for a situation, e.g., a radar confused by a metal bridge or a camera blinded by glare.

ISO/PAS 8800: Safety for AI/machine-learning parts, e.g., how to prove a pedestrian-detection network is good enough.

🔁 The safety lifecycle (V-model)

Left side goes down: Item definition → HARA → Functional safety concept → Technical safety concept → Hardware/Software design.
Right side goes up: Unit tests → Integration tests → Vehicle validation → Safety case (the argument + evidence that the car is safe) → Release.

💡 Key difference from safety: A hazard doesn't get smarter over time, but attackers do. That's why TARA is revisited throughout the car's life.
Module 3

HARA: Hazard Analysis & Risk Assessment

HARA answers: "If this function misbehaves, how could someone get hurt, and how seriously must we protect against it?"

Walk through the 7 steps

The three dials: S, E and C

S: Severity How badly could people be hurt?
S0 No injuries
S1 Light/moderate injuries
S2 Severe, survival likely
S3 Life-threatening or fatal
E: Exposure How often is the car in that situation?
E0 Practically never
E1 Very rare
E2 Rare (<1% of driving time)
E3 Sometimes (1–10%)
E4 Often (>10%)
C: Controllability Can a normal driver avoid harm?
C0 Always controllable
C1 Simply (≥99% of drivers)
C2 Normally (≥90%)
C3 Hard or impossible (<90%)

💡 Memory trick: Add the numbers S + E + C. 10 = D, 9 = C, 8 = B, 7 = A, 6 or less = QM. If any of them is 0, it's QM.

✂️ ASIL decomposition: splitting a tough requirement

An ASIL D requirement can be shared between two independent parts (e.g., a main and a backup channel), each built to a lower level:

  • D = C(D) + A(D)
  • D = B(D) + B(D)
  • D = D(D) + QM(D)

The "(D)" means the parts still remember the original goal was D. Independence (no common cause of failure) must be proven.

🎯 What makes a good safety goal?

Written at vehicle level, technology-neutral, with an ASIL, a safe state and a Fault Tolerant Time Interval (FTTI).

Example: "Avoid unintended steering torque above 3 Nm for longer than 50 ms. ASIL D. Safe state: steering assist switched off with driver warning."

Where they connect

🔗 Shared item definition
Both analyses start from the same description of the system.

Damage scenarios with "Safety" impact link to HARA hazards. An attack is just another way to cause the hazard.
⬅️ HARA → TARA
Safety mechanisms and safety-critical messages become important security assets.
